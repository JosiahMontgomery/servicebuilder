Temperate Coniferous Forest

The Temperate Coniferous Forest biome is one of the most common in Washington State, with it covering practically all of the lower elevation Cascade Mountain Range, the majority of the Olympic Peninsula, Willapa Hills, Puget Lowlands, and Blue Mountains. However, even in this biome there is extreme biological variety, with three main zones.

First is the Coastal forests, which are the wettest in this biome, and are characterized by extreme moss growth, and Western Redcedars and Western Hemlocks as the common trees. These forests can be incredibly thick, though not to the extent of the Temperate Rainforests located in thsi similar region.

The West side Cascade Mountains and Puget Lowlands are cool, wet forests, filled with Pacific Silver Fir and Western Hemlocks. Generally thick forests; less so compared to the forests nearer to the Coast, but compared to most forests throughout the continente they are very much so. Lots of underbrush, etc.

Crossing over to the east side of the Cascade Crest the forests change dramatically, from fir/hemlock thick wet landscapes to the dry, thinner forests dominated by Ponderosa Pines and Conifers.

There are also many smaller sub-biomes, such as the Riparian Coniferous forests near rivers and streams, and the changing forests in the Cascades as altitude increases. Overall, Washington has very diverse and unique forests, with many old growth trees over 500 years old, as well as some truly enormously thick trees that rival those in the Redwood national park, which honestly is a crappy national park not worth visiting.

Temperate Steppe

Temperate Shrub-steppe is a semi-arid biome, covering much of the Plateaus of Eastern Washington. It is characterized by rolling plains of sagebrush and bunchgrass, experiencing hot summers and cold winters.

Sub-Alpine / Alpine

The Sub-Alpine biome is defined by altitude; it starts generally as the trees become much shorter and scarcer at higher mountain elevations, and ends once you get high up out of treeline. The Alpine Biome begins where Sub-Alpine ends, and is defined by all area above the treeline (until you get to permenant ice/rock). Generally, these two biomes are characterized by scarcer, shorter Alpine trees, and eventually no trees; also, by Alpine meadows and grasses.

Temperate Rainforest

The Temperate Rainforests of Washington are a unique biome primarily located in the valleys of the Olympic Mountains, and consist of old growth forest systems that recieve over 140 inches of rain annually. The Olympic Rainforest Complex is the second largest rainforest in North America, only beaten by the Tongass National Forest in Alaska.

The rainforests of Washington consist of extremely high precipitation, moderate temperatures, extreme biodiversity of mosses, ferns, and old-growth Sitka Spruce, Douglas Fir, and Western Redcedar. The record for most rainfall in this region was 185 inches in 1931 year at the Wynoochee Oxbow.

Grasslands & Savannas

This Biome could in some ways be split in two, as it consists in Washington of three very distinct and rare biomes that are found Puget Sound Lowlands, but do not cover any large area.

The first is the very rare Puget Prairies, a biome unique entirely to the South Puget Sound area. According to sources, these praires once covered around 150,000 acres in Washington, but today has dwindled to roughly 10% of this area.

The other biome is Oak Savannas, which can be found in Washington, Oregon, and California, but are very rare indeed. They exist in multiple specific locations, also in the South Puget Sound region, and consist of the native Garry Oak alongside grasslands.

The third is The Palouse, a drive of 4-5 hours from the Puget Sound area, in far Eastern Washington. These rolling hills are truly a distinct region.

Highland Tundra

Washington's Highland Tundra is practically contained solely in the vast Pasayten Wilderness, a huge tract of land in the Eastern North Cascades, with enormous plateau-like regions at impressively high elevations. They are generally above the main treeline, and hence are large, desolate tracts of land in the far remote, consisting of high alpine grasses, recieving little precipitation. Truly a unique region.

Mediterranean

Yes, Washington does in fact have a Mediterranean biome. Located in the San Juan Islands, an Archipelago of 400-800 islands in the Salish Sea (number of islands depends on time of day, due to tide). Unlike Washington's varieties of other biomes, the San Juans have a warm-summer Mediterranean biology due to the Olympic Mountain's rainshadow effect, featuring cool, wet winters and warm, dry summers. The islands are characterized by maritime meadow habitats and rocky balds. It also has certain unique flora and fauna such as sharp-tailed snake and white-topped aster, as well as various seabird and marine populations.

Do note, however, that there is both a Mediterranean Biome, as mentioned here, which refers to the actual biology (or in other words, the species of plants and animals), as well as a Mediterranean Climate, referring to the climatology of the regino (temperature and precipitation as the common defining factors).

Permanent Snow / Ice

Pretty self-explanatory. This biome is only possible in places either so close to the poles of the Earth that the temperatures drop extremely low, or so high in elevation; Washington state has some of both, being far north enough and having high enough elevations that it has quite a bit of this biome. This biome has very little biology in general, as nearly no plant or animal life exists here, especially in Washington state, where these areas are unsurvivable for life. There is approximately about 600 square miles of this biome in WA, though it is hard to track this exact number
